But, for some, the anti-AI sentiment goes a lot deeper than marketing and about a lack of demonstrated value. T. The average person thinks of LLMs as the thing their kids use to cheat on school assignments. AI may have made their life more convenient on the margins, but that is the margins. So, you end up asking communities to accept these data centers and sanction the local tentacle of a technology that is already making people far away very rich, but which has not provided the country writ large with any tangible goods. The problem is the substance, not the messaging. Another point along these lines that I've seen some make is that even the things that AI does really well, i.e. increasing productivity, aren't necessarily things that the average person wants. In a recent podcast appearance, writer Ben Thompson said that every 10 years Silicon Valley relearns that consumers do not want to pay for software, and that consumers do not care about being productive. I think when it comes to AI, it's not just that consumers don't care about being more productive, it's that when you combine it with the entire AI industry saying that AI is going to come for your jobs, and tons of CEOs using it as an excuse for layoffs that they were going to make anyway, productivity is just another way of saying, "You're going to make me give away more of my labor that I don't own." Still, if it were just dislike of AI, even that would be different than what it actually is. AI is the inheritor to the mantle of a 15-year decline of people's perception of tech in general. Gavin Purcell writes, "My feeling is that at least 75% of this negativity against AI is about big tech at large. Over the last 10 years, big tech has had the following very bad stories and many more: election manipulation, screen time worries amongst kids, directional content rabbit holes that radicalize people, killing the media business like TV, movies, and news, polarizing communities against one another, massive profits versus local communities getting poorer, woke companies making cultural choices, and on and on and on. I think AI and data centers specifically has finally let a long-simmering anger have real-world, in data centers literal real-world targets." In the same post I was reading from before, Tim O'Reilly again writes, "Data centers have unfortunately become the symbolic representation of the asymmetric upside for a very narrow tech elite and a class that are untrustworthy. Now, keep in mind, Americans' trust of elites and institutions right now is at an all-time low. According to a recent Gallup poll, the average percentage of US adults that express a great deal or quite a lot of confidence in nine major US institutions, including the church, the military, the Supreme Court, banks, public schools, newspapers, Congress, organized labor, and big business, is down to its lowest point ever at 27%. Just one in four adults express confidence in the major institutions of American life. The number when it comes to big business is just 17%. And when you're already mistrustful of institutions, the way that data centers have done business, i.e. behind closed doors, outside the eye of the public, using tools like non-disclosure agreements, is almost tailormade to increase that mistrust. NBC recently published a piece called how NDAs keep AI data center details hidden from Americans, and this I think is a massive massive hard to overstate part of this problem right now. Technology moving ahead represents change you can't control. Change you can't control makes people feel less agency in their lives. And so, the fact that already people are feeling less agency, and then they don't even have a chance to see what's going on and how these decisions are being made, that's not something that anyone, but particularly Americans, is going to take kindly to. Jasmine Sun has done some great reporting around this recently. And in a recent long-form piece that has got her featured on numerous major news outlets and podcasts, she argued that this is actually a huge part of the problem. In what I think is the most important part of that entire 6,000-word tome she writes, "My conversations made me wonder if the technical content of data center deals is mostly besides the point. Closed-loop cooling systems? I don't believe them. Paying millions in taxes? I don't believe them. Creating a thousand jobs? I don't believe them. I hear a reflexive skepticism of every claim. Rather, the way that AI companies engage with communities, forcing NDAs, dangling billion-dollar promises, pushing environmental externalities far away from AI's wealthy user base, resembles a classic story about dark money and politics. Your politicians are being bought by billionaires and gigantic corporations to screw you over. It's not Skynet, it's the oligarchy. Writes Jonathan Keepman, data centers are a proxy for largely inarticulate concerns over tech and the emerging likelihood that AI is going to radically change our way of life, much in the same way the internet has, and driven at least in part by two populist intuitions. One is that the further human value is abstracted out from the production of tangible things, the worse it is, and also that if the value of that thing isn't easily explained, if it's mechanics are opaque or even unknowable, then I, the average guy, am probably somehow getting screwed. This doesn't mean that the concerns are fake or unjustified, only that they are often misdirected. As you can probably tell, while there are entire sections of the critiques and concerns that I either disagree with or that I think are just based on total misinformation, I think that the only realistic starting point for finding a way forward is understanding the real concerns that exist and trying to find solutions for those specific problems. Unfortunately, the solutions that have come up so far are largely in the form of moratoriums. Interconnected Capital has a digital map that tracks moratoriums following 97 county bans, 93 city bans, and 28 town bans. Recently, my state of New York became the first state to officially impose a data center moratorium, but now the tide is moving quickly. In the last couple of weeks, we've had great examples of formerly extremely data center bullish governors reversing their opinions and basically competing to have the strictest rules in the country. I'm referring, of course, to Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro and Texas Governor Greg Abbott, and a recent memo from the National Republican Senatorial Committee about the liability of data centers, specifically in Ohio, but in other parts of the country as well, seems to have gotten the entire Republican Party on board with being anti-data center in one fell swoop. Michigan Republican Tom Barrett is running an ad decrying in his words politicians cozying up with big tech billionaires to force data centers on our communities, despite his voting record in favor of data centers before. Republican candidate for Wisconsin Governor Tom Tiffany is going out of his way to paint his Democratic counterpart David Crowley as the great champion of data centers. Interestingly, Tiffany seems to be trying to combine the culture war anti-data center sentiment and anti- environmentalism all in one fell swoop, posting on X this week, "David Crowley's data center plan would destroy at least 100,000 acres of Wisconsin land. His Beyonce approach calls for data centers powered by 100% solar, wind, and battery storage. You get a data center and thousands of acres of solar panels in your backyard." Heat maps Matthew Zeitlin perfectly sums it up. Every statewide election now is two people who supported data centers 5 minutes ago saying that their opponent supports data centers. So, where do we go from here? Firstly, there is work to be done and good work being done on trying to combat the misinformation. A lot of the memes about water usage, for example, are somewhere between out of context, i.e. understanding what these big numbers of gallons of water used actually mean relative to all other uses, or just straight-up factual inaccuracies that become memes. For example, in Karen House's book Empire of AI, she miscalculated the amount of water used by a factor of 1,000. And while she has admitted the error, the misprint remains in circulation and contributing to people's takes on this. A similar process is happening around the concern that, as Bloomberg put it in an article last year, AI data centers are sending power bills soaring. But when people actually dig in, it appears like there's actually no real statistical correlation between where electricity prices are going up the most and where data centers are located, which of course doesn't mean that it's a non-issue, but it's a question of specific data center design and the environmental design around it, not some rule of nature that data centers are going to jack up your electricity prices. Unfortunately, that baseline trust gap that we discussed limits the ability for these rational arguments to find purchase. Again, from a Puck News poll, when asked how convincing the respondents found certain arguments in favor of data centers to be, the factual fixes ranked pretty low. For example, they asked specifically about the excessive water claim being based on that math error, acknowledging that it has since been corrected, and only 8% of people found that very convincing and only 20% of people found that somewhat convincing. Around the argument that data centers would create thousands of high-paying jobs for local community members, again, people just don't believe it. Only 12% found that very convincing and 23% found that somewhat convincing. One has to think that that's not people being dismissive of the value of high-paying jobs. It's saying, "Yeah, right. We'll talk when I actually see those jobs." The most convincing argument that they could find was data centers generating money in local tax revenue for schools, roads, and other services. This still wasn't a knockout, with only 14% finding it very convincing and another 28% finding it somewhat convincing. And when it comes to the part of this that could be attributed to the messaging around AI and jobs, thankfully, that has shifted lately. I tend to think it's on the too little, too late side of things, but what's more important than just the messaging changing is the fact that the evidence shows that the argument that AI was going to cause massive job loss was always wrong in the first place. In other words, Ford deciding to rehire what they call graybeard engineers does a lot more than CEO's saying, "Oops, I was wrong about job loss." ever could. There are also better sources for certain types of pro data center messaging than the tech companies or data center builders themselves. It would certainly be overstating it to say that all labor unions were on the side of the data centers, but if you are looking for one area where they have had an enormous positive effect right away, it's in the trades. And frankly, the unions, who are both seeing those benefits but who are also members of those communities, are uniquely positioned to navigate the nuance and complexity of the situation without surrendering the realness of the value that is coming from these things being built. And as the threat of moratoriums gets bigger, these groups are getting louder. The International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers recently said that proposed data center bans and moratoriums in New England, quote, "Threaten to block billions of dollars in potential investment and will eliminate critical job opportunities for IBEW members." If statewide bans are implemented, New England will lose countless jobs and infrastructure investments created by the current boom in AI technology. Going on, they said, "Why this matters to the IBEW? Data centers create substantial work opportunities for electrical workers in industries tied to AI and energy buildout. Strong labor standards on data centers will raise wages, improve working conditions, and protect local workers. Investment in union apprenticeship and training programs will help build the next generation of skilled IBEW workers. Data center projects can generate significant local tax revenue and infrastructure investment. Total bans discourage investment and send good jobs to other areas." And when you peel back the comment section-style anger that surrounds this issue, there are folks out there who are unwilling to surrender to any extremes of any side in the conversation. In a response to author Noah Rothman asking, "Is there no cure for the data center hysteria?" Nebraska Congressman Don Bacon said, "Yes, speak the truth. Data centers can be built smartly to conserve water and expand energy production. We can't win the AI war if we're going to wage war against data centers. Data centers have made a positive impact in Sarpy County, where I live." And very high on the agenda for the tech companies has to be actually not just explaining how local communities could benefit from hosting data centers, but how they are in practice. For example, some recent research is showing areas where, for example, Dominion Energy customers in Virginia actually saw slightly lowered electricity rates because of data centers and the associated load growth. Quincy is a small farming town in Washington that started attracting data centers back in 2006. They continued adding data centers over the subsequent two decades and currently have 30 in the municipality. There are around 900 engineering and construction jobs that work directly on the data centers and according to analysis from Washington State, another four to six jobs in construction, retail, and local services for every one of those 900 data center jobs. The data centers contribute 57% of Quincy's property taxes and have funded public buildings including a $15 six-lane swimming pool, a $120 state of the art high school, and a large indoor sports complex that is currently under construction. The poverty rate in Quincy has fallen from 29.4% in 2012 to 6.2% in 2024. Pat Haley, a former city administrator, called it the Quincy miracle. He said Quincy was a farming and ag town. That was your career path if you were going to stay. Data centers opened up what could have been a low opportunity town into this place with brand new everything. Another example is Loudoun County in North Virginia. It's home to Data Center Alley, which is the region with the highest concentration of data centers on Earth. It houses over 200 facilities representing about 14% of global capacity. Data centers take up about 3% of the county's land and around 40% of its budget. However, the data center industry is a massive net contributor to the economy. According to a state audit, around 74,000 Virginia residents work in the industry representing 5.5 billion in labor income. It is also the wealthiest county in the US. However, unlike other ultra-wealthy counties, it also has one of the lowest residential property tax rates in the country. Loudoun County has lowered its property tax rate every year over the past 10 years and now charges just 0.8%. And that's largely because data centers are footing the bill. They now contribute around 42% of local tax funding up from 32.8% in 2024. What's more, in a recent study, the Chamber of Progress found that compared to the other three counties in Northern Virginia, the county delivers above average spending on schools, libraries, and public safety. Fairfax County, for example, charges around 35% more in property taxes while spending around 45% less on public services. The whole thing here comes down to a simple equation. Does a community feel that the value that they get from a data center being in their community is more than the cost that it incurs? What more can the developers add to make the equation come up positive for the community? Clearly, the data center builders are getting the memo. Meta has recently launched a billion-dollar fund to invest in local initiatives around where they're building. In their Pike County, Ohio, facility, OpenAI has announced $40 billion from OpenAI directly for priorities that they say will be defined by the local community. And that is in addition to what they they will be the hundreds of millions of dollars in state and local tax revenue over its lifetime. They're also giving away 84 million dollars in Code X credits to approximately 844,000 Ohio college, community college, and technical students. And in further signs that they're finally getting the memo, Microsoft earlier this year announced that they had made the decision to end the use of NDAs with local governments in order to increase transparency. But what about those politicians? Well, if they do it well, they have an opportunity to be the intermediaries that minimize the negatives and deliver some incredible positives. Reports are coming out that West Virginia is exploring how they could use data centers to eliminate their income tax altogether, which is a plan that Americans for Tax Reform President Grover Norquist seems very excited about, suggesting that all states should require that tax payments by data centers will go to reduce property taxes at the local level and income taxes at the state level. Krieg you on X said, "This is a fun exercise. How large a data center would your town need to replace your property taxes?" Doing some back-of-the-napkin math for a 20,000 person town using the average rate that US local governments collect in property taxes, they found that, quote, "In short, being generous, you could drop your property taxes entirely with a data center that doesn't even hit the hyperscale threshold of 250 MW." But, and this is a big but, none of this can happen if the policy chosen is moratoriums. To be clear, moratoriums do nothing to solve the problem. They simply kick the can down the field. They feel good to people whose core emotional consideration is actually just trying to stop change, but they do not actually address any problems or create any opportunities to find the good that might be won. And here's a real narrative violation that you're not going to see in clickbait headlines. People, shockingly, aren't stupid. They have real concerns about data centers that are, yes, based on a variety of different issues, which ask any two people and you might get three different answers for why they don't like data centers. But it turns out, if you ask them, they'd rather have plans that addressed those specific problems than just shut everything down. A recent Morning Consult poll found that voters prefer data center rules to a moratorium. When asked what their preferred approach to data center construction would be, 23% said a moratorium on all new projects, but 57% said clear rules, let responsible projects be built. When asked about a project that meets disclosure, cost, and public review requirements, 24% yes, almost a quarter, said stop it from being built, but 58% said allow it to move forward. When asked about the companies not just paying their way, but supporting a broader revitalization, i.e. requiring companies to pay for the grid upgrades their facilities need, just 13% opposed that and 78% supported it. The irony of this issue is that it is maybe the most winnable political compromise issue that I have ever seen. These data centers are absolutely mission-critical to the people who are building them in the larger infrastructure that relies on them. It does not matter ultimately, and I believe will not matter in the long run, that it might have been easier before when people weren't paying attention to do things as they had always been done through NDAs and a very small number of gatekeepers, rather than having to go get the approval of the actual communities. But you better believe, if given an actionable path forward, especially one where the biggest consideration pretty much just comes down to spending more money in the community that those data centers are built in, everyone involved with the data centers will jump at the chance. So if you're looking for positive places to go, look for the politicians who are jumping on this issue by actually offering a path forward. Wisconsin governor candidate again, David Crowley, recently put out a press release on data centers. The press release reads, "As governor, Crowley would let communities decide for themselves whether they want a data center at all. And if they say no, that decision would be honored. If a community says yes, the developer must commit to full transparency by banning NDAs, provide 100% renewable energy, which will combat the amount of coal we burn, cover 100% of the cost of infrastructure and grid upgrades, require contributions to the state's focus on energy fund, which could be used to bring down utility rates for Wisconsinites statewide, build the data center with union labor project labor agreements and community benefit agreements, create good paying family supporting jobs, and protect local water and our environment as a whole. I think this is the direction we're going to see where the smart politicians create checklists that the developers can actually meet, but do so on the basis of community agency. And that goes for Democrats and Republicans. Ohio candidate for governor Vivek Ramaswamy at the beginning of the month pledged to Ohioans that if a data center was built in their community that they would no longer have to pay for their home's electricity, that they would pay lower property taxes, and that the data center would be required to abide by all air and water quality standards. Yes, we need to build more data centers, but if we can actually come through this contentious period and build them on these sorts of foundations where people feel heard and they actually understand how they're benefiting directly from them, that is an unfathomably better foundation for the future than what we might have had. I wouldn't be surprised to see this issue get worse before it gets better. At least when it comes to popular perception and media headlines. But boy, when politicians start realizing that they can deliver all these benefits and be the heroes that connect the dots by simply picking up on templates that have and are working for other communities, that's when I think we'll see a change for the better.
