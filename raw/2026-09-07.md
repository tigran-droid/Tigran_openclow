Anthropic says its Claude model just produced the first fully computer checked proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, working largely on its own for 11 days and writing what the company calls the longest formal math proof ever assembled.

📐 Fermat scribbled his famous claim into the margin of a notebook in 1637, insisting he had a proof too large to fit, then died without ever showing it. It took 358 years before Andrew Wiles published a real proof in 1995, a 129 page argument that came only after he had to fix a hole discovered right after his public 1993 announcement.

🤖 According to Anthropic, dozens of Claude agents worked in parallel, writing definitions, proving small results and stacking them into bigger theorems, coordinated by a tool called Prove2Me built by researcher Tianyi Peng's group at Columbia. The company says the agents proved more than 29,500 intermediate theorems along the way, producing roughly 13 million lines of Lean code, and that the effort built on parts of the existing formalization work from Imperial College London's own FLT project.

⚖️ Kevin Buzzard, the mathematician who has led that human project since 2024 with funding secured through 2029, reviewed Claude's proof and confirmed it holds up using nothing but math's basic axioms. A job his own team was still grinding through, Claude reportedly wrapped up in eleven days, following what Anthropic describes as a simplified route through Wiles's argument.

🧭 Why it matters: this isn't Claude discovering new mathematics, Wiles already proved the theorem three decades ago. What Anthropic claims to have built instead is a machine checkable receipt for that proof, at a moment when mathematicians say they're increasingly swamped with unverified proofs, AI written ones included, arriving faster than anyone can check them by hand.

What strikes me most in this story isn't really the speed, it's the kind of work that got handed off to a machine.
