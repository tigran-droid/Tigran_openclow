Six test videos. More than 12,000 paid operations.

That is what it took to turn a simple AI idea into something that almost worked.

The brief sounded easy: paste a video link, detect whether it was generated by AI, return a verdict.

The first version looked finished after little more than an hour.

It was not finished.

It confidently called an obviously synthetic video real. Eight hours of autonomous testing later, the system still stalled. A specialist detection service improved the result, but disagreed with Gemini. The builder eventually made the specialist signal take priority and got better answers.

Then the economics broke.

Six videos had consumed more than 12,000 service operations. The builder could publish the code, but could not responsibly open the product to everyone without knowing what each user would cost.

This is the part of AI transformation that the demo economy hides.

Generating software is becoming cheap.

Operating reliable software is not.

The real product includes evaluation data, confidence rules, third-party dependencies, exception handling and a unit-cost model. If any one of those is missing, you have a prototype wearing a product’s clothes.

So ask a harder question after the demo:

What happens on the hundredth real case, and who pays for being wrong?

AI regulation is moving from principles to receipts.

OpenAI is now calling for mandatory, capability-based national safety requirements.

Not another voluntary promise.

Common testing. Independent assessment. Cybersecurity requirements. Incident reporting. Clear measures for when development should slow or stop.

It also endorsed four California bills covering independent safety assessors, AI-auditor standards, protection for young users and safeguards against AI-enabled biological threats.

There is an obvious conflict here: a frontier lab is helping shape the rules that will govern frontier labs.

So do not mistake advocacy for proof.

But do not miss the direction either.

The standard is becoming observable behaviour.

Can the system respect authorization? Can it avoid sharing data too broadly? Can it be monitored across a full task? Can an independent party inspect the evidence? Is there a duty to report when a model crosses a security boundary?

Those questions do not belong only in Washington or Brussels.

They belong in procurement meetings now.

Every company deploying agents should be able to show its capability tests, permission boundaries, intervention rules and incident path before the system touches consequential work.

A policy says what you intend.

Evidence shows what the system actually did.

━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━

Even if AI progress stopped today, the disruption would not.

Ethan Mollick put the remaining runway at at least a decade.

That sounds extreme until you look at how technology actually enters a company.

The model arrives first.

Then people learn what it can do. Workflows are rebuilt. Interfaces improve. Costs fall. Policies catch up. Managers discover that a task was never one task at all, but a bundle of judgment, administration, checking and handoffs.

Only then does the organisation change.

Mollick also described rapidly deskilling himself on annoying tasks he neither wants nor needs to remain good at.

That is useful. But it creates a management decision most companies have not made deliberately:

Which skills should disappear?

Which must remain because they carry judgment, resilience or accountability?

If AI drafts the analysis, can your team still recognise a false premise? If an agent runs the process, can someone recover it when the dependency fails? If nobody practises the underlying task, where does the next generation of experts come from?

“Use more AI” is not a workforce strategy.

Decide what to forget.

Decide what must stay human.

Then redesign the work around that choice.


[6/7] 📄 ARTICLE

Cheap AI Prototypes Are Creating Expensive Operating Problems

Building an AI application is becoming astonishingly fast. Building one that can be trusted, afforded and operated at scale is not.

Today’s evidence makes that gap hard to ignore. OpenAI says GPT-6 Astra can work through ordinary desktop applications, complete complex professional tasks with fewer retries and reduce unintended computer-use outcomes. Meanwhile, a practical attempt to build an AI-video detector took more than ten hours, a paid specialist service and repeated human intervention before it produced acceptable answers.

The prototype is no longer the hard part

The detector began with a reasonable idea: paste a social-media link and find out whether the video is synthetic. An AI coding system produced a polished application quickly. The interface worked. Reports appeared. Confidence scores looked authoritative.

The underlying verdict was wrong.

An obviously generated video was classified as probably real. The system assigned high confidence using weak logic. After hours of autonomous testing, it still could not reconcile two components: Gemini’s visual review and a specialist detection service.

This is becoming a familiar enterprise pattern. The visible layer arrives first, so the organisation mistakes presentation for capability. A workflow can look complete while its most important decision remains unreliable.

Testing changes the product

The builder did the right thing next: he tested known synthetic and real videos repeatedly. The system changed its confidence logic. The specialist signal was given more weight. Longer videos were handled. Disagreement became visible instead of being hidden beneath a confident answer.

Those changes were not cosmetic bug fixes. They defined what the product actually was.

OpenAI’s own Astra announcement points in the same direction. The strongest claims are not that the model can produce more text. They are claims about completed work: a 20% improvement in pass rate on workflows lasting more than five hours, around 20% more bugs caught in one customer test, and fewer unsupported conclusions in another. OpenAI also reports 89% fewer unintended outcomes than GPT-5.6 Sol on its internal computer-use safety benchmark.

These are vendor and customer claims, not universal facts. But the unit of measurement is right. Pass rates. Errors. Unsupported conclusions. Unintended outcomes. Completed tasks.

Cost appears after the demo

The detector eventually became more accurate. Then a second failure appeared.

Six test videos consumed more than 12,000 operations from the specialist service. The builder could not predict the cost of letting the public use it. The code could be shared. The service could not responsibly be opened.

This is why token prices tell so little about AI economics. The real cost includes retries, external services, evaluation, human review, monitoring and failures. A cheap model can support an expensive workflow. A premium model can be cheaper if it finishes the task with fewer calls and less correction.

OpenAI prices Astra from $10 per million input tokens and $50 per million output tokens. That is useful information, but it is not a business case. The business case is cost per accepted result under real operating conditions.

Controls must be part of the design

As models operate browsers and desktop applications, mistakes stop being drafts and start becoming actions. OpenAI now offers controls to restrict approved websites and applications, manage uploads and downloads, and require confirmation before consequential actions.

At the policy level, the company is also calling for mandatory capability-based rules, independent assessments and serious-incident reporting. Whatever one thinks of a vendor advocating regulation for its own industry, the direction is clear: capability without observable control is no longer enough.

The practical response for leaders is simple.

Before approving an AI workflow, require a representative test set, a named acceptance threshold, a full cost model, explicit permission boundaries and a recovery path. Run the ugly cases, not only the demo cases. Measure disagreement, retries and human interventions. Expand autonomy only when the evidence improves.

The code may now arrive in an afternoon.

Trust still has to be engineered.

▸ The Prototype-to-Production Cost Checklist
Angle: A practical method for finding the costs that appear only after an AI demo works.
Outline: Define the accepted result in operational terms. Build a representative test set. Count model calls, retries and external-service operations. Price human review and exception handling. Test the worst plausible usage pattern. Set a stop condition before public rollout.
Why now: Today’s video-detector experiment showed how six tests could consume more than 12,000 paid operations even after the application appeared complete.

▸ The Organisational Deskilling Map
Angle: Companies should choose deliberately which skills AI may replace and which capabilities must remain healthy.
Outline: Break roles into task bundles. Separate low-value repetition from judgment. Identify recovery skills for failed automation. Protect apprenticeship paths for scarce expertise. Test whether people can still challenge outputs. Review the map as models and workflows change.
Why now: Current AI may keep transforming work for years through diffusion, even without another major model breakthrough.
